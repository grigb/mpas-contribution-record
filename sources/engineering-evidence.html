Dated engineering evidence summary

This source contains permitted summaries of retained engineering and acceptance records through September 14, 2026. The underlying source trees, patches, test logs and internal acceptance records remain private. A reader can inspect this summary; this edition does not make those underlying tests independently inspectable or report a new test run. Public merge evidence is listed separately in Public references.

Multi-Party Action Security (MPAS) binds an exact requested action to approvals, checks the governing policy and records the reported result. A proposer requests an action; signers review it; a verifier checks permission; a credential adapter can execute permitted operations for a target that does not verify MPAS itself.

Reading untrusted input consistently

The broader strict-input repair extends duplicate-field rejection beyond the merged signed-approval fix. It covers responses from services, coordination notifications, public-key data, plugin input, and signature parameters. It also rejects names that look different in their encoded form but decode to the same name. The practical result is that the system rejects ambiguous input before using it for routing, authentication, or an authorization decision. Valid inputs and established error shapes remain supported.

Verifying the intended action and authorized signatures

The verifier repair checks the trusted target application and execution rules before trusting payload or policy data. It validates the complete action and approval structures, requires a consistent signed representation, binds signatures to authorized keys, and checks approval time and expiry against trusted inputs. It rejects contradictory approve/reject decisions by the same signer and makes replay scope explicit where an action identifier is not globally unique.

These checks protect the connection between what was reviewed and what may run. A valid signature alone is insufficient if it applies to the wrong action, uses an unauthorized key, has expired, or is interpreted through untrusted execution rules. The accepted change includes corrected signed fixtures—fixed examples of input and expected results used in tests—and a controlled test clock so that time-dependent checks are repeatable.

Preserving the meaning of approval policies

The policy repair validates the complete policy before using its fields. Malformed input and inherited object properties cannot supply authority. Signer membership, proposer exclusion, thresholds, overrides, and action rules are evaluated together. Nested rules meaning “all these conditions” and “any of these alternatives” retain their meaning through the policy engine and the services that consume its responses.

This also makes the remaining work shown to a signer accurate. Already satisfied conditions disappear from the pending requirements, while viable alternatives remain available. Excluding the proposer happens before deciding whether enough eligible signers exist. An impossible requirement is reported as unreachable instead of presenting a misleading approval request. Independent review found boundary defects in an earlier candidate; the accepted package retains those failures and their corrections.

Defining the signer's job and keeping metadata from granting credentials

The signer work supplies a proposed interoperability profile and matching discovery, pending-review, approve, and reject behavior. The reviewer receives the exact action, payload, approval requirements, reference, and expiry. Invalid review data is rejected before submission. A response received after a submission is checked without silently submitting again or claiming success prematurely.

The same work separates information shown to the reviewer from actual provider permission. The field called expectedAuthority describes expected authority for review; it does not grant it. Real service permissions remain in trusted deployment configuration. This removes the ambiguity in the older field name and aligns the specification, generated tools, plugins, and reference data. It is specification and interoperability work as well as code.

Recovering honestly from temporary failures

When policy evaluation is temporarily unavailable, the workflow must not mistake that for a final rejection or ask for approvals to work around it. The recovery change keeps the exact action package and response, remembers which phase needs retrying, and retries only when due. Cancellation and expiry still take effect. Its own evidence covers a new engine instance reusing retained state; process-restart evidence comes from the separate durable-dispatch work.

The coordination repair distinguishes delivery of a request, collection of approvals, the verifier's decision, and execution. It validates complete responses, retains the original proposer and stable submission identities, and keeps pending, expired, cancelled, rejected, temporarily unavailable, and executed-without-result states separate. That prevents a caller from presenting “request received” as “action approved” or “action finished.”

Preventing a restart or race from repeating an action

The durable-dispatch change records authorization to dispatch against the complete action identity and payload hash. The demonstration implementation uses its existing SQLite database to record that decision atomically and keep the stored resolution immutable. Preparation that can fail happens before recording execution; transmission follows the record.

If the process stops with an unresolved execution, recovery marks it indeterminate instead of sending it again. Competing responses return the stored winner. Retained checks include separate processes, forced termination around transmission, target-action counts, and recovery of signed results. Those checks support the named restart and concurrency behavior; simulated storage failures are not a claim of physical power-loss testing.

Making receipts independently checkable

An execution receipt is a signed report of an outcome. The receipt repair adds a public verification function that requires trusted action and payload inputs and the authority of the expected issuer. It checks structure, signed bytes, outcome, action binding, time, and the issuer's relationship to the signing key. Failed verification returns no trusted payload. Receipt creation also binds the stated issuer to the key actually used.

This makes it possible for another component to reject a forged, misbound, or unauthorized receipt. It does not establish that the external service performed every claimed effect, and it adds no retry or execution behavior. Those limits are part of the contribution, not exceptions to hide.

Keeping sign-in and transport within the intended authority

The delegated-login work binds a session to the intended authorization, owner, and sharing policy. It rejects mixed-up callbacks, supports the required client modes, prevents competing token refreshes, remembers when a new sign-in is required, and handles expiry and logout. The corrected logout path checks the complete binding before revoking credentials, deleting state, or reporting success. Provider behavior in the retained tests was synthetic; live provider compatibility is a separate claim.

The transport repair prevents lower-level behavior from bypassing the reviewed decision. Requests cannot grant themselves transport permission. Denied operations stop before credential reads or target effects. Authentication errors, insufficient permission, and redirects do not silently trigger a retry, a wider permission request, or a second destination effect. This work connects policy to the actual point at which the external action is sent.

Controlling generated tools and returned information

The result-disclosure change requires the generated tool's reviewed policy to govern what result data may leave the local system. Denied results remain local across a new request, later polling, and recovery; allowed results retain their receipts. Copying the policy at construction prevents a caller from changing authority later by mutating the object it supplied. Historical stored data is preserved while new disclosure is filtered.

The bridge generator turns application definitions into tools exposed through the Model Context Protocol, an interface used by agents. Its repair validates registry and plugin inputs before use, checks content identity, limits schema-processing resources, preserves distinct capability errors, and signs direct requests by default. An explicit trusted exception remains possible where the defined direct-only mode allows it. Retained evidence distinguishes runs using a published library from runs using the local repaired candidate.

Carrying the authority change into applications and developer setup

Changing a protocol field is incomplete if applications still use its old meaning. The first application package migrated the existing authority metadata and recomputed the plugin identities and references affected by those edits. Later accepted work completed the Slack and Outlook declarations and repository validator coverage. The September 11 independent source acceptance confirms the full migration of 24 application plugins, 29 authority positions, and 52 references that use those plugin identities across the application and central MPAS repositories. That acceptance covers synthetic providers and software behavior without relying on a language model. It supersedes the catalog's earlier partial migration for the exact combined private candidate; it does not prove a live deployment of each application.

The separate Outlook adapter work accepts a credential for one operation, constrains access to the intended resource, and avoids acquiring an account, selecting a personal profile, or using a personal credential cache. The complete candidate also includes a managed local-process runtime and installation guidance. Controlled provider targets and synthetic credentials support these checks. The recorded six actual-account Outlook tests were not executed.

Developer-facing work made release dry runs keep prereleases out of the stable channel, checked documented public entry points and runnable examples, aligned the generator's library version with its manifest, and exercised independent adapter startup and shutdown. Two focused test corrections addressed temporary database path aliases on macOS and missing version arguments in generator rejection tests without weakening production checks. These changes make the work easier to reproduce and integrate; they are useful deliverables even though a dry run is not a release.

Later repairs and the combined private delivery

The work continued after the catalog's September 10 snapshot. One repair rejects impossible calendar dates in action timestamps instead of allowing a date parser to normalize them into another date. Another lets an eligible signer inspect a pending request before choosing approve or reject; inspection does not sign or submit, and signing still requires an explicit decision.

The September 11 combined candidate joins accepted repairs, conformance fixtures, application references, local-process runtime and setup guidance. Historical packages overlap and cannot be counted as separate fixes or applied as an unrestricted patch stack.

The later proposer-instruction correction addresses how an agent handles the response to a protected call. The instructions distinguish an immediate result from a real task or deferred handle, permit read-only observation only with a real handle, and prohibit duplicate protected calls, self-approval, and premature success. The accepted change covers six documentation and skill files. The September 14 package acceptance preserves the historical package count and expressly does not establish model compliance or a passed live-agent scenario.

These additions matter because they correct both execution rules and how an agent is instructed to use them. They also show why the old package count is not a reliable measure of the full current work. Exact source, scope, and acceptance are more useful than a larger headline total.

Verification work and what its results mean

The verification program developed regression cases that reproduce defects, positive controls that preserve valid behavior, and conformance fixtures that check whether implementation behavior matches the protocol. It also assembled source maps, contained before-and-after files, patches, and acceptance references so that a reviewer can identify exactly which candidate was checked. That is necessary work for a security-sensitive contribution: a passing result on a different library or a patch that changes unrelated files is not enough.

The September 11 independent source acceptance records 190 declared, independently executed, and passing conformance fixtures on the combined source. It accepts non-model operability and conformance within the stated synthetic-provider boundary. This is a substantive result on a named fixture set, not a new total of unique tests across the project. Earlier focused tests overlap full suites and successive candidates. This report therefore does not add historical test counts. Package-integrity acceptance separately checks the material handed to a reviewer; it does not substitute for running the product in its intended environment.

The September 14 acceptance record used for this report records zero accepted scenarios out of the required 17 real-agent scenarios. That program covers real proposing and signing roles, valid approval, rejection and expiry, tampering, duplicate submission, restart, uncertain outcomes, credential separation, and matching receipts. It remains separate from the accepted instruction repair. Final integrated conformance and final disposition of the complete finding program also remain unaccepted in that record. These limits govern technical claims; the remaining product work retains its existing scope.

The September 14 local finding-disposition map accounts for all 55 original program entries. It records 52 accepted component repairs and three accepted partial conformance increments: testing the main approval-checking library's required verification rules (N06), providing reusable protocol and application-integration test cases (N37), and rejecting a claim of test success when required cases were not run (issue 19). These three increments have accepted evidence for software behavior without a language model; they are not completed conformance requirements. Every entry still records final integrated acceptance as false. The matching acceptance record binds this exact local map and preserves those limits.

Research, review, and presentation material already produced

The research is organized into a summary of seven decision questions and ten subject chapters: the competitive field; differentiation; buyers and money; feasibility; security and agent trust; liability; company, intellectual property, and standards; regulation; product shape; and what the recorded demonstrations run. The questions connect technical work to the choice of business, rather than leaving it as disconnected research.

Three reusable report-and-presentation packs were completed: external research, product and commercial direction, and delivery and readiness. Each addresses a different conversation. The research pack explains findings and limits. The product pack separates possible users, offerings, adoption routes, and commercial assumptions. The delivery pack explains what exists, which evidence is required next, and what depends on it. Their dates matter: a completed presentation is not a current claim that its technical status has remained unchanged.

The contribution catalog adds plain feature descriptions, issue and proposed-contribution mappings, a test catalog, detailed test definitions, and source notes. The private review packages include exact source changes, their parents and dependency order, retained checks, and proposed review text. Together these make a contribution reviewable without requiring reconstruction of the whole investigation.

The final technical report and presentation are different deliverables. They are intended to summarize accepted complete technical results and remain downstream of that acceptance. This progress report makes the existing work visible now; it does not rename unfinished final proof as complete.

Evidence limits

Component repair, a checked delivery package, a public merge, an integrated test, a live-agent scenario and a deployment are separate evidence. The September 14 summary records 52 accepted component repairs and three written conformance entries with accepted partial offline work. All 55 final integrated finding acceptances remained pending. The same cutoff records zero accepted real-agent scenarios out of 17. These are dated records, not a claim about every later system or deployment.

No historical test counts are added together. This edition provides no private patch, source coordinate, reproduction recipe, customer claim or publication approval.
